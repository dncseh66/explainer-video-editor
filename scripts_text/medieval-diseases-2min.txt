All right, let's go. Number 10, epidemic typhus. The admissions hall of the Sadiki Hospital in Tunis was an ordinary place in 1909. Patients arrived in their own clothes, were stripped, bathed with soap and hot water, and given clean hospital linen.

Their old clothing was bundled and carried to the laundry. It was routine hygiene, repeated hundreds of times without a second thought. But Charles Nicolle, director of the Pasteur Institute in Tunis, noticed something strange about that routine.

Typhus patients infected the staff who received them, the orderlies who undressed them, and the workers who washed their clothes. Once they were bathed and on the ward, they infected no one at all.

The war for Granada was fought by an army that lived in its clothes. In the campaigns of 1489 and 1490, Spanish soldiers slept in tents and earthworks, packed shoulder to shoulder for warmth. Blankets passed from the sick to the well without a second look.

Wool tunics were worn for weeks without being removed. Linen shirts stiffened with sweat and wood smoke. At night, the camps were never fully silent because thousands of men were scratching. They scratched at the waistband, the armpits, and the collar until the skin broke and their fingernails came away dark. Then, in one tent and then another, the scratching stopped. A man would lie still, staring at nothing, too far gone and fevered to feel the itch at all.

The cause of that itch was something every soldier knew and no one feared. The body louse is roughly the size of a sesame seed. It does not live on the skin. It lives in the clothing, clinging to the seams and folds that sit closest to the body's warmth. It climbs down to feed several times a day, then climbs back into the fabric to digest. The female lays her eggs along the threads and glues them to the fibers so firmly that shaking a garment does nothing. A single unwashed shirt can shelter hundreds of them. To a medieval soldier, lice were as ordinary as mud, an unpleasant fact of sleeping in the field.
